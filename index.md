# Hello! 

Welcome to Prob Blog, a probability blog. Here, we present human-generated expositions on topics related to probability theory.

# Our Goal

The goal of Prob Blog is three-fold: to encourage human-generated mathematics content, to provide intuitive and introductory explanations for people seeking to learn more about probability, and to offer opportunities for young mathematicians to learn and explain new or familiar mathematical topics.     


# Why Human-generated?
Lately, mathematics has become a playing ground for Large-language models (LLMs). This effort has been driven largely by large companies such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and others, whose interest in mathematics is due exclusively to their interest in showcasing the strength of some new model. It is no question that these models can now answer research-level mathematics problems, including the recently solved Navier-Stokes Millennium Problem. Despite the obvious success of these companies with respect to solving research-level mathematics problems, their impact on the mathematics community has been crisis-inducing. 

From an outsider's perspective, the goal of mathematics might appear to be that of solving problems. This is not the reality; in general, we only care partially about whether a given conjecture is true; what we really care about is the journey required to get to that conjecture, or perhaps more importantly, the understanding of the journey. LLMs short-circuit this process and leave us humans with a weak understanding of the result itself and the tools used to get there. When an LLM solves a problem, it is, for one, generally very difficult to digest and, two, often nearly impossible to see how it arrived there. In contrast, human-generated mathematics (at least should) offer a strong exposition of the mathematics and the construction of new well-understood ideas that will hopefully eventually be applied to the sciences. I think this is best summarized by Erdős's metaphor of "The Book" or "Gods Book", wherein the most elegant proofs of every theorem are contained; the goal of mathematicians is not just to prove Theorems, but to attain or approximate these proofs contained in the book. Our goal here is similar: not just to present results but to construct digestible and elegant expositions of such results which in turn allow us as humans to talk about mathematics either with other mathematicians or researchers ready to make use of mathematical results. Without this clean exposition or understanding of results, the purpose of mathematics becomes tainted: we have a collection of results for which nobody really understands (including mathematicians), and thus has no real use. The counterargument to this might be that the job of human mathematicians is to take the ideas of an LLM and to articulate them to other mathematicians or researchers. I think this is extremely naive; reading vs. writing a paper are two dramatically different degrees of understanding, i.e., there is a reason we hire researchers to teach the topics they research. One might argue that researchers in other fields will also be agents, and thus one agent communicating to another is not an issue, but this has two main issues: for one, are we really willing to let these machines automate research to such an extreme degree when the safety and control of these agents are clearly major issues; and for another, we miss the point of the sciences and mathematics, that is, to actually understand the world around us. Finally, let's suppose these machines are now safe and can communicate what they are doing effectively to humans; we are then again missing a major aspect of mathematics and science. That is the joy of finding things out! There is such a vast array of human knowledge in the world that we simply won't touch any but a small corner of it, and so most of our enjoyment comes from actually finding these things out for ourselves, applying the epistemological principles that unfold our knowledge.

I have not even touched on the impacts these models are having on education, which is a separate issue in its own right. This blog runs on a philosophy aligned with The Association for Human Mathematics (AHM: https://www.ahmath.org/), wherein we are trying to encourage human-generated mathematics content and research. You can find out more about these ideas in the preprint seen here: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02859.
